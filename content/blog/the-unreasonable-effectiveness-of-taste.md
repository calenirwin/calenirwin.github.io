Everyone can ship now.

A solo developer with the right AI tools can build in a weekend what took a team of five a quarter to produce three years ago. The barrier to creating software has collapsed. We're living through the most dramatic reduction in the cost of execution in the history of technology.

And yet most of what gets built is still mediocre.

This isn't a paradox. It's a signal. When everyone has access to the same tools, the tools stop being the differentiator. What's left is the person using them — specifically, the quality of their judgment. Their taste.

## What taste actually is

Taste isn't aesthetic preference. It's not about picking nice fonts or knowing which shade of blue converts better. Those are symptoms.

Taste is the ability to look at ten options and know which one is right without being able to fully articulate why. It's the willingness to kill something that's good because it isn't great. It's the instinct for what matters and what doesn't — applied relentlessly, across every decision, at every level of the stack.

Steve Jobs didn't design the iPhone. He couldn't write Objective-C. What he could do was sit in a room with ten prototypes and say *that one* — and be right. He could feel when something was off by two pixels. He could sense when a feature, no matter how technically impressive, didn't serve the whole.

That's taste. It's pattern recognition trained on quality.

## Taste in building products

Every product is a thousand small bets. Which features to build. Which to cut. How the onboarding should flow. What the empty state should say. Whether the settings page needs twelve options or three.

Most builders optimize for completeness. Add more features, cover more use cases, handle more edge cases. This is the default gravity of software development — things accumulate. Codebases grow. Products bloat.

Taste is the opposing force. It's the discipline to ask: *does this serve the user, or does it serve my anxiety about missing something?*

The best products feel inevitable. You open them and there's nothing to figure out, nothing that feels unnecessary. That inevitability isn't the absence of effort — it's the result of enormous effort applied to subtraction. Someone made a hundred decisions to remove things so that what remains feels obvious.

That someone had taste.

## Why AI amplifies taste

Here's where it gets interesting for builders.

AI gives you infinite generation capacity but zero judgment. You can spin up ten landing page variations in ten minutes. You can generate five different data models before lunch. You can prototype three approaches to the same feature in an afternoon.

But you still need to know which one is right.

AI is leverage, and leverage is a multiplier. It amplifies whatever you already have. If you have good judgment, AI makes you devastating — you can explore the solution space faster than ever and converge on the right answer with terrifying efficiency. If you don't have good judgment, you just build the wrong thing faster.

This is why the next decade belongs to small teams with strong taste. Not because AI replaces people, but because AI removes the execution bottleneck that used to require people. A two-person team with great judgment and AI tools can outship a fifty-person team that ships by committee.

The constraint has shifted. It used to be: *can you build it?* Now it's: *should you build it, and is this the right version of it?*

## Developing taste

Taste isn't innate. It's built, like a muscle. But the training method is different from what most people expect.

You don't develop taste by studying design systems or reading about product strategy. You develop it by exposing yourself to quality — then building, shipping, and paying attention to what happens.

**Read widely.** Not just tech blogs. Read literature, philosophy, history. The patterns that make a good novel work are the same patterns that make a good product work: clarity of purpose, economy of expression, respect for the audience's intelligence.

**Study things you admire.** When you use a product that feels right, slow down and ask why. What did they include? More importantly, what did they leave out? Reverse-engineer the decisions.

**Ship and iterate.** Taste without reps is just opinion. You need the feedback loop — build something, put it in front of people, watch what happens, adjust. Over hundreds of cycles, your intuition calibrates.

**Learn to see.** Most people look at a product and see features. People with taste look at a product and see decisions. Every pixel, every word, every interaction is a choice someone made. Once you start seeing the decisions, you start developing opinions about them. That's taste emerging.

## The moat

Technology is commoditized. Capital is abundant. Talent is globalized. AI is compressing the execution advantage that used to separate teams.

What's left? Judgment. The quality of your decisions about what to build and how to build it.

The founders who win in the next decade won't be the ones with the most engineers or the biggest funding rounds. They'll be the ones who consistently make better decisions about what matters — who have the taste to build something that feels inevitable.

Build your taste like you'd build any other skill. Deliberately, consistently, with high standards and honest feedback.

It's the only moat that compounds.
